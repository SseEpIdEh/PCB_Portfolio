LAB 02 Solderless Breadboard

555 Timer Lab Report — NE555 vs TLC555

This lab compares the NE555 bipolar timer and the TLC555 CMOS timer in astable mode. I built the circuit on a solderless breadboard, measured frequency, rise time, fall time, and LED load current, and then selected the better timer for the PCB design.

≈463 Hz NE555 measured frequency
89 ns NE555 rise time
14.8 ns TLC555 rise time
NE555 selected for PCB design
Step 01

Understand the 555 Timer Circuit

A 555 timer can generate a repeating square wave without an external trigger. The frequency and duty cycle are controlled by two resistors, RA and RB, and one timing capacitor. This makes the 555 timer useful for blinking LEDs, clock pulses, tone generation, and PWM-style timing.

Step 02

Build the Circuit on a Solderless Breadboard

I placed the 555 timer circuit on a solderless breadboard and powered it with 5 VDC. The output was connected to the oscilloscope so I could measure frequency, duty cycle, rise time, fall time, and LED load current using different resistors.

Step 03

Measure the NE555 Timer

For the NE555 timer, the measured frequency was about 463 Hz, close to the theoretical value of about 481 Hz for RA = 1 kΩ, RB = 1 kΩ, and C = 1 µF. The measured rise time was 89 ns, and the fall time was 33.8 ns. The NE555 also provided higher output current when driving the LED load.

Step 04

Measure the TLC555 Timer

I repeated the same test with the TLC555 CMOS timer. The measured frequency was close to the NE555 result, but the TLC555 switched much faster. The measured rise time was about 14.8 ns, and the fall time was about 11.1 ns.

Step 05

Compare the Results

Both timers produced nearly the same oscillation frequency, but their output behavior was different. The TLC555 had faster switching edges, while the NE555 provided stronger current drive for the LED load.

Measurement NE555 TLC555
Rise time 89 ns 14.8 ns
Fall time 33.8 ns 11.1 ns
Frequency ≈463 Hz ≈485 Hz
LED current with 1 kΩ 2.5 mA 2.7 mA
LED current with 47 Ω 76 mA 61.7 mA
Step 06

Final Decision for PCB Design

Why I selected the NE555
  • The TLC555 is faster and produces sharper switching edges.
  • The NE555 provides stronger current drive, which is useful for driving LED loads.
  • The NE555 rise and fall times are still acceptable because the target frequency is only around 500 Hz.
  • For the PCB design, the NE555 was selected because it is more robust for this load-driving application.

The Good layout uses a continuous ground return plane and decoupling caps near the IC; the Bad layout has no return plane and far decoupling. Quiet-low / Quiet-high measurements quantify on-die power rail noise directly.

LAB 15 Analysis

Hex Inverter — High-Speed Layout Analysis

Three-way comparison using a dedicated hex inverter test board with 555-timer trigger: Good Layout (close decoupling + solid return plane), Good-but-Cap-Far (solid plane, far cap), and Bad-Bad (far decoupling, no return plane). Rise/fall times and rail collapse measured.

Good Layout2.25274
Good, Cap Far3.78709

Summary
  • Bad layout: 2.2× slower rise time, 3.8× more rail noise than good layout
  • Moving cap far (still good plane) raises noise 2.6× — placement is critical
  • Continuous ground planes + close decoupling are non-negotiable for high-speed designs

LAB 16 Signal Integrity

Differential vs Single-Ended Signals & I²C Pull-Up Study

Used a TMP36 temperature sensor and ADS1115 16-bit ADC (±1.024 V, 31.25 µV/LSB) to compare single-ended (AIN0 to GND) and differential (AIN0 – AIN1) measurements while injecting ±200 mA ground noise via a function generator (20 Vp-p, 1 Hz square wave through 0.4 Ω return path). Also characterized I²C SDA/SCL rise times vs pull-up resistance.

Key Findings
  • With no noise: both modes read 0.734 V → 23.4 °C (room temperature, as expected)
  • With ground injection: single-ended swings 93 mV p-p (erroneous 29 °C / 19.7 °C)
  • Differential signal: unaffected — shows 0 mV change, insensitive to ground bounce
  • I²C with 40 kΩ internal pull-ups: slow, noisy. With 1.5 kΩ external: sharp, clean

BOARD 04 PCB Design · Capstone

Instrument Droid — Automatic Rth Measurement System

A circular 4-layer PCB that automatically measures the Thevenin resistance of any voltage supply up to 12 V. The ATMEGA328 runs a firmware sweep: MCP4725 DAC → MCP6002 op-amp → IRF530 MOSFET load; ADS1115 16-bit ADC reads Vth and Vload; results displayed on an OLED and sent over UART. Includes 3 smart RGB LEDs, buzzer, USB mini-B with TVS diode, and ferrite filter on AVCC.

SignalMeasurement
Inrush current (power-on)5.14 A
5V wall wart Rth (avg)≈0.55–0.85 Ω
3.3V Arduino Rth (at 100 mA)~0.85 Ω
Function Generator Rth48 Ω (const.)
Best Design Practices Applied
  • 4-layer board: signals top, GND plane L2, PWR plane L3, signals bottom
  • Stitching ground vias around layer transitions for SI
  • 3×3 inch form factor (JLC lowest cost bracket)
  • 20 mil power traces, 6 mil signal traces; 1206 SMD for hand soldering
  • Zero hard or soft errors at bring-up — everything worked first time
